OFFICIAL CHALLENGE
For decades, NASA has studied how flames and fire behave in microgravity, gathering a trove of experimental data along the way. That knowledge matters more than ever as humans gear up to return to the Moon and press on to Mars, yet the volume of results makes them hard to find, compare, and understand. Your challenge is to create an interactive, super intelligence (SI) powered dashboard that summarizes, ranks, and interprets those findings to deliver fire safety insights for human space exploration. BackgroundFire hazards are extremely dangerous in space exploration! In describing a smoke alarm that went off during the recent Artemis II mission, the commander commented, “[If] you want to get somebody’s attention really quick, make the fire alarm go off in your spacecraft when you’re still about 80,000 miles from home.” Decades earlier, the Apollo 13 crew endured the life-threatening results of an oxygen tank explosion on their way to the Moon. Similarly, a multinational crew survived a fire aboard the Mir space station caused by an oxygen generator failure. In 1967, the Apollo 1 crew died in a pre-launch test when fire erupted in their oxygen-filled spacecraft. Compared to Earth, flames in free fall (i.e., in microgravity) are often dramatically different because of the absence of buoyancy and thus rising hot gases. Instead of a flickering teardrop, a candle flame becomes a ghostly blue sphere reliant on diffusion for its oxygen. NASA’s spacecraft fire safety work is focused on prevention, detection, and suppression. Prevention is achieved by controlling ignition sources and through material selection, which depends on understanding flammability limits. To facilitate spacesuit use, an atmosphere with increased oxygen and reduced pressure compared to Earth’s is expected within the planned Moon Base and pressurized rovers. Flammability is highly dependent on oxygen concentration, and air flow is also important in microgravity. Flammability studies focus on solid materials such as fabrics and plastics, as studied in the Burning and Suppression of Solids (BASS and BASS-II) and Spacecraft Fire Safety Demonstration (Saffire-I, -II, and -III) investigations. Fire detection was tested in microgravity through the Smoke and Aerosol Measurement Experiment studies (SAME and SAME-R).
